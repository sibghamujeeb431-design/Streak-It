# Gram Staining

Gram staining is a differential staining method. It separates bacteria into Gram-positive and Gram-negative groups based on differences in their cell-envelope structure. It does **not** identify an exact species.

## Reagents and their roles

1. **Crystal Violet** — primary stain. It initially colors all cells purple/violet.
2. **Gram's Iodine** — mordant/fixing reagent. It forms a complex with crystal violet that is harder to wash out.
3. **Decolorizer** — removes stain from some cells. This is the most critical step.
4. **Safranin** — counterstain. It colors cells that lost the primary stain pink/red.

## Step sequence

1. Select a prepared fixed slide.
2. Apply Crystal Violet.
3. Apply Gram's Iodine.
4. Decolorize carefully.
5. Apply Safranin.
6. Observe under the microscope.
7. Classify as Gram-positive or Gram-negative and explain.

## Expected results

- **Gram-positive** bacteria appear purple/violet.
- **Gram-negative** bacteria appear pink/red.

## Common mistakes and consequences

- **Wrong reagent or wrong sequence** — results are unreliable; the stain combination does not produce a valid differential result.
- **Over-decolorization** — can make Gram-positive cells appear falsely Gram-negative (pink/red). This is a false-negative Gram-positive result.
- **Under-decolorization** — can make Gram-negative cells retain the primary stain and appear falsely Gram-positive (purple/violet).
- **Skipping a stage** — the result is usually uninterpretable.
- **Misinterpreting the final color** — purple/violet means Gram-positive; pink/red means Gram-negative.

## Key teaching points

- Sequence matters.
- Decolorization is the critical step.
- Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria have different cell-envelope structures.
- The final color is what you observe, but a wrong technique can make it misleading.

# Streak Plate Method

The streak plate method isolates individual bacterial colonies from a mixed sample. Cells are progressively spread across the agar surface so later regions receive fewer cells. The goal is to obtain isolated colonies for further study.

## Required equipment and materials

1. **Bacterial sample** — the mixed culture you want to separate.
2. **Sterile agar plate** — the solid growth medium.
3. **Sterile inoculation loop** — the tool used to transfer and spread cells.
4. **Bunsen burner** — used to flame-sterilize the loop between regions.

## Step sequence

1. **Prepare** — select the bacterial sample, a sterile agar plate, and a sterile inoculation loop.
2. **Streak** — streak the sample across four plate regions in a quadrant pattern. Flame the loop before moving to a new region and let it cool.
3. **Incubate** — allow the plate to grow under suitable conditions.
4. **Observe** — examine the colony pattern.
5. **Interpret** — decide whether the result shows isolated colonies and explain why.

## How quadrant streaking works

- **Region 1** — heaviest inoculum; cells are densely packed.
- **Region 2** — a few cells are carried over from region 1.
- **Region 3** — fewer cells are carried over from region 2.
- **Region 4** — fewest cells; isolated colonies are most likely here.

Flaming the loop between regions kills remaining cells, so each new region starts with only what was carried over from the previous streak.

## Expected results

A successful streak plate shows a gradient of growth: dense growth in the first region and progressively more isolated colonies in later regions. Isolated colonies indicate good separation.

## Common mistakes and consequences

- **Heavy carryover** — too many cells are dragged into later regions, so colonies remain crowded instead of isolated. This produces poor separation.
- **Missed flaming** — failing to flame the loop before a new region can transfer too many cells and may introduce contamination.
- **Ineffective pattern** — not streaking all four regions or using an uneven pattern gives patchy, unreliable separation.
- **Contamination** — using non-sterile equipment or poor aseptic technique introduces unwanted microbes, seen as off-pattern satellite colonies.
- **Hot loop** — touching the agar with a hot loop kills cells and may damage the medium.

## Key teaching points

- The goal is progressive separation, not just drawing lines on the plate.
- Colony density should decrease from region 1 to region 4.
- Isolated colonies are useful for further study, but one colony does not automatically mean one species.
- Sterile technique and loop flaming are essential for a valid result.

# Antimicrobial Susceptibility Test (AST)

The disc diffusion (Kirby–Bauer) antimicrobial susceptibility test measures whether a bacterial strain is inhibited by specific antimicrobial drugs. Paper discs containing known concentrations of each drug are placed on a lawn of bacteria spread over Mueller–Hinton agar. During incubation, each drug diffuses outward from its disc while the bacteria grow. Where the drug concentration stays high enough to inhibit growth, a clear "inhibition zone" appears around the disc.

## Required equipment and materials

1. **Bacterial sample** — the standardized suspension of the strain being tested.
2. **Sterile Mueller–Hinton agar plate** — the standard medium for disc diffusion testing.
3. **Sterile swab** — used to spread an even lawn of bacteria across the agar surface.
4. **Antibiotic discs** — paper discs impregnated with a fixed amount of each drug.

## Step sequence

1. **Prepare** — select the bacterial sample, a sterile Mueller–Hinton plate, and a sterile swab.
2. **Inoculate** — swab the entire plate surface in multiple directions to form an even lawn.
3. **Place discs** — place 3–5 antibiotic discs onto the lawn, spaced well apart.
4. **Incubate** — incubate the plate so the drugs diffuse and the lawn grows.
5. **Interpret** — measure each inhibition zone and classify the response.

## How inoculation technique affects results

- An **even lawn** gives uniform bacterial growth, so zone edges are sharp and zone diameters are reliable.
- An **uneven lawn** (for example, a single quick swipe) grows patchy. Zone edges become distorted or irregular, and zone diameters become unreliable because the drug is diffusing into an inconsistent density of cells.

## Standardized interpretation criteria

Zone diameters are measured in millimeters across each inhibition zone. Each antibiotic has its own published breakpoint table that maps a measured zone diameter to a category for a given organism group:

- **Susceptible (S)** — the zone is at or above the susceptible breakpoint; the drug is likely effective at standard doses.
- **Intermediate (I)** — the zone falls between breakpoints; the drug may work only at higher exposure or in body sites where the drug concentrates.
- **Resistant (R)** — the zone is at or below the resistant breakpoint; the drug is unlikely to be effective.

Breakpoints differ from drug to drug and from organism group to organism group, which is why the same zone diameter can mean different things for different drugs.

## The most common misconception — correct it actively

**A larger inhibition zone does NOT automatically mean a stronger or better antibiotic.** Zone size reflects how far the drug diffuses through the agar under the test conditions (its concentration, molecular properties, and solubility) combined with how susceptible the organism is. Interpretation is only valid when the measured zone is compared with the standardized breakpoint criteria for that specific drug and organism. Never rank antibiotics against each other by raw zone size.

## Common mistakes and consequences

- **Uneven lawn** — patchy growth distorts zone edges and makes zone diameters unreliable.
- **Discs placed too close together or overlapping zones** — zones merge and cannot be measured accurately.
- **Too few discs** — an incomplete picture of the strain's susceptibility profile.
- **Reading zones before proper incubation** — zones have not fully formed and measurements are meaningless.
- **Judging by zone size alone** — conclusions must come from the standardized breakpoints for each drug, not from comparing zones against each other.

## Key teaching points

- Diffusion and growth happen at the same time; the zone is where inhibition won.
- The medium, inoculum density, and incubation conditions are standardized on purpose — interpretation only works if the technique is consistent.
- Susceptible, Intermediate, and Resistant are interpretations against breakpoints, not descriptions of zone size.
- Gentamicin 10 µg example breakpoints used in this simulation: Susceptible ≥ 16 mm, Intermediate 13–15 mm, Resistant ≤ 12 mm.

# Serial Dilution & Pour Plate

Serial dilution progressively reduces the concentration of a bacterial sample across a series of tubes. Each tube contains the same volume of diluent, and a fixed volume from the previous tube is transferred to the next. This creates a predictable concentration gradient (10^-1, 10^-2, 10^-3, 10^-4, 10^-5). Aliquots from each dilution are poured onto agar plates to obtain countable colonies for viable cell counting.

## Step sequence

1. **Prepare** — select the bacterial sample, dilution tubes with diluent, and inoculation tools.
2. **Dilute** — transfer sample from the original culture into the first dilution tube, mix, then transfer to the next tube. Repeat across all five tubes.
3. **Pour Plate** — select a dilution tube (ideally one expected to give countable colonies), pour an aliquot onto an agar plate, and mix thoroughly.
4. **Incubate** — allow the plate to grow under suitable conditions.
5. **Interpret** — count colonies on the plate and calculate the original cell concentration using the dilution factor.

## Dilution math basics

Each dilution step reduces concentration by a factor of 10. If the original culture has 10^7 cells/mL, then:
- 10^-1 dilution: 10^6 cells/mL
- 10^-2 dilution: 10^5 cells/mL
- 10^-3 dilution: 10^4 cells/mL
- 10^-4 dilution: 10^3 cells/mL
- 10^-5 dilution: 10^2 cells/mL

To calculate CFU/mL from colony count: (colony count) × (dilution factor) × (volume correction factor). For example, 150 colonies on a 10^-3 plate from 1 mL → 150 × 10^3 = 150,000 CFU/mL.

## Choosing the right dilution for plating

A countable plate typically has 30–300 colonies. Fewer than 30 makes the count statistically unreliable; more than 300 colonies are too crowded to count accurately and may have merged. Plates that are too concentrated (no isolated colonies) or too dilute (no colonies) are not suitable for calculation.

## Common mistakes and consequences

- **Incomplete mixing between transfers** — dilution factors become unpredictable and concentration calculations are invalid.
- **Carrying over too much volume** — breaks the 10-fold dilution assumption and throws off the math.
- **Skipping a dilution step** — creates a gap in the concentration gradient and may miss the countable range.
- **Plating the wrong dilution** — if the plate is too concentrated or too dilute, colonies cannot be counted reliably.
- **Incorrect CFU calculation** — forgetting to apply the dilution factor or volume correction leads to wildly inaccurate cell counts.

## Key teaching points

- Serial dilution is about predictable, proportional reduction — each step is exactly 10× more dilute than the previous.
- Mixing between transfers is essential for accurate dilution factors.
- The ideal plate for counting is in the middle of the gradient — not the most concentrated, not the most dilute.
- CFU/mL requires applying the dilution factor math correctly.

# Biochemical Testing

Biochemical testing identifies bacteria by detecting specific enzymes or metabolic pathways through reactions in prepared media. Different bacteria have different metabolic capabilities, so the **full combination** of test results is compared against reference identification criteria. No single test alone can identify a species.

## The five tests and their reactions

1. **Catalase (H2O2)** — Tests for catalase enzyme. Positive = bubble formation; Negative = no bubble.
2. **Oxidase (TMPD)** — Tests for cytochrome oxidase. Positive = purple color change; Negative = no color change.
3. **Indole (Kovacs)** — Tests for tryptophanase. Positive = red ring at surface; Negative = no red ring.
4. **Citrate (Simmons)** — Tests for citrate utilization. Positive = blue color; Negative = green color.
5. **Urease (Christensen)** — Tests for urease enzyme. Positive = pink color; Negative = yellow color.

## Step sequence

1. Select the unknown bacterial sample and the set of 5 biochemical test tubes.
2. Inoculate each test tube with the unknown sample using a sterile loop.
3. Submit for incubation to allow reactions to develop.
4. Observe and record the reaction in each test tube.
5. Interpret the pattern of positive/negative results to identify the organism.

## Key misconception to correct

**A common mistake is relying on a single positive test result to identify a species.** Identification is pattern-based across multiple tests — the full combination of results matters, not any single test alone. Always encourage students to look at the entire profile.

## Common mistakes and consequences

- **Incomplete inoculation** — skipping a tube or not changing the loop between tubes can contaminate results and make the profile unreliable.
- **Misreading reactions** — confusing positive and negative indicators leads to wrong identification.
- **Over-reliance on one test** — leads to misidentification; the correct approach is to consider all 5 tests together.

## Key teaching points

- Each test reveals one metabolic capability; together they narrow down species identity.
- Identification is pattern-based — you need the full combination, not any single result.
- The same organism can test positive for some tests and negative for others — that's normal and expected.
